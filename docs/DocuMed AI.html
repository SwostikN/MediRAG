03 · THE CORE PROBLEM
WHY LLMS HALLUCINATE
Hallucination, defined
An LLM does not retrieve facts —
it predicts the next likely token.
With no grounding mechanism, the model fills gaps with what sounds right. In open chat that produces plausible prose. In medicine it produces plausible harm.
01Probabilistic next-token prediction.
02No factual verification mechanism.
03Training data ages quickly in medicine.
04Confidence is not calibrated to truth.
Prediction surface vs ground truth
Confidence ≠ Accuracy Plausible ≠ True Grounding ≠ Memory

06 · THE TURNING POINT
WHY NAIVE RAG IS NOT ENOUGH
The turning point
Retrieval alone does not eliminate hallucination.
Models hallucinate even with context.
FAILURE MODE 01
Wrong context retrieved
Top-k pulls a paper about adult dosing when the query was about a child. The LLM answers fluently — but on the wrong evidence.
FAILURE MODE 02
Right context, ignored
The retrieved chunk says "contraindicated in pregnancy"; the model answers based on its prior, not the evidence.
FAILURE MODE 03
Stale evidence
The chunk is from 2014. Guidelines changed in 2023. The answer is technically grounded — and technically harmful.

07 · RESEARCH FOUNDATIONS
WHAT WE TOOK · HOW WE USE IT
From the literature, into the system
Six ideas we borrowed —
and where they live in DocuMed AI.
01RETRIEVAL CORE
RAG backbone for our LLM
We build on the parametric-plus-non-parametric pattern: instead of asking the LLM to recall medical facts, we make it read retrieved chunks and answer from them.
Lewis et al. — RAG · NeurIPS 2020
02VERIFICATION
A check stage after generation
The hallucination taxonomy (intrinsic vs extrinsic) convinced us that retrieval alone isn't enough — so stage 4 of our pipeline verifies the answer against its sources.
Ji et al. — Hallucination Survey · ACM CSUR 2023
03VERIFICATION
Sentence-level NLI verifier
We use a small DeBERTa-v3 NLI model to score entailment between each generated sentence and its cited chunk. It's fast, cheap, and avoids a second LLM call per sentence.
He et al. — DeBERTa-v3 · ICLR 2023
04RETRIEVAL
Hybrid BM25 + dense via RRF
Our retriever runs BM25 and dense vector search in parallel, then merges with Reciprocal Rank Fusion — robust to score-scale differences between the two lanes.
Cormack et al. — RRF · SIGIR 2009
05DOMAIN
MedCPT for the dense lane
Generic embeddings miss medical synonyms and rare drug names. We swap them for MedCPT in the dense retrieval lane so biomedical terms cluster correctly.
Jin et al. — MedCPT · Bioinformatics 2023
06SAFETY
Fail-closed refusal threshold
Selective-prediction theory says abstaining beats answering wrong in high-stakes QA. Below our calibrated confidence band, the system refuses instead of generating.
Kamath et al. — Selective QA · ACL 2020
08 · WHAT IS RAG
RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED GENERATION
Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Don't ask the model to remember.
Ask it to read.
Why it helps
Forces the model to answer from text it can read — not from blurry parametric memory.
What it doesn't fix
If retrieval is wrong, generation is wrong. RAG moves the failure — it doesn't remove it.
09 · RAG IS NOT ENOUGH
FOUR FAILURE MODES
Even with retrieval, hallucinations leak through
Plain RAG still fails in four ways.
We fix each one.
01
Wrong documents come back
Embedding similarity ≠ medical relevance. The top-k chunks aren't really about the question.
→
OUR FIX
Hybrid retrieval + reranker
BM25 + dense + cross-encoder.
02
The model says things the source doesn't
Even with good chunks, the LLM adds sentences that no retrieved passage actually supports.
→
OUR FIX
Sentence-level NLI check
Every claim entailed, or it's flagged.
03
The evidence is out of date
A 2014 guideline scores high on similarity but has been superseded by newer recommendations.
→
OUR FIX
Freshness-weighted ranking
Recent guidelines win ties.
04
It always answers — even when it shouldn't
Plain RAG has no way to say "I don't know." In medicine, a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer.
→
OUR FIX
Fail-closed refusal
Abstain below a confidence threshold.

12 · SAFETY PIPELINE
FAIL-CLOSED · 5 STAGES
Five-stage safety pipeline
Every stage can refuse. Silence is a feature.
A request flows through five gates. If any one of them isn't confident, the pipeline stops — the user gets a referral, a follow-up question, or an honest "I don't know" instead of a wrong answer.
01
GATE 1
Red Flag
Detect emergencies
If the question describes something life-threatening — chest pain, stroke signs, heavy bleeding — the system stops and tells the user to go to the ER right away.
e.g. "crushing chest pain since morning"
REFUSE → ER PATH
02
GATE 2
Intent & Intake
Understand the user
The system figures out what the user is really asking, and asks back for basic details — age, current medicines, allergies, pregnancy — that change the answer.
e.g. "are you pregnant? on any meds?"
REFUSE → ASK FOLLOW-UP
03
GATE 3
Hybrid Retrieval
Find evidence
The system searches our medical library two different ways — by keywords and by meaning — then merges the results to pick the most relevant passages.
e.g. ACOG bulletin · Cochrane review
REFUSE → INSUFFICIENT
04
GATE 4
Grounded Generation
Answer from text
The LLM is told: only use what's in the retrieved passages, and put a citation next to every claim. No making things up.
e.g. every claim tagged [source · §]
REFUSE → "I DON'T KNOW"
05
GATE 5
Verify & Validate
Sentence-level check
A second model reads each sentence of the answer and checks: "is this actually backed by the source?" Anything unsupported gets softened or removed.
e.g. unsupported lines silently dropped
REFUSE → REDACT
FAIL-OPEN (typical chatbot)
UNSAFE
When the chatbot isn't sure, it answers anyway — and gives a confident wrong reply. Dangerous in medicine.
FAIL-CLOSED (DocuMed AI)
SAFE
When DocuMed AI isn't sure, it refuses, asks for more details, or sends the user to a clinician. Saying nothing is safer than guessing.
13 · VERIFICATION
CONFIDENCE-AWARE · NLI
Strongest innovation
Every sentence is fact-checked.
After the answer is written, it's broken into individual sentences. Each sentence is compared against the source text by a second AI model that decides one of three things:
Backed by source
KEEP
The sentence clearly follows from the cited passage. Show as-is.
Partially supported
SOFTEN
Reword with hedging ("may", "some studies suggest") so it doesn't overclaim.
Not supported
REMOVE
Drop the sentence entirely. Better silent than wrong.

14 · FRESHNESS
EVIDENCE AGES
Freshness-aware retrieval
Medicine has a half-life.
A 2014 guideline and a 2024 guideline can disagree — and only one is current. The system gives newer sources more weight, so the answer reflects today's medicine, not last decade's.
2014 dosing guideline
weight 0.07
Old, often superseded — pushed down the ranking.
2019 review paper
weight 0.37
Still useful background, but not the latest word.
2024 ACOG update
weight 0.81
Current standard of care — surfaces first in results.
FRESHNESS WEIGHT BY YEAR
Older than 10 years → weight near zero unless it's the only source. Newer than 2 years → near full weight.

15 · NEPAL CONTEXT
LOCALIZATION MATTERS
Why localize
A US triage answer is wrong in rural Nepal.
Care tiers, drug availability, emergency numbers, distance to specialists — none of this generalizes. DocuMed AI is aware of where the user is and what they can actually do.
Self-medication is the norm
Refusing to answer isn't enough — the system must point to a real next step the user can take today.
Specialists cluster in cities
A rural user's nearest hospital may be hours away; "see a cardiologist" is not advice — it's a dead end.
Different drugs, different names
Nepali pharmacies stock different brand names and formulations than US guidelines assume.
Emergency is 102, not 911
Hardcoding US numbers in a refusal can cost minutes. The system uses local emergency numbers.

19 · LIVE WALKTHROUGH
QUERY → ANSWER
Real query, traced end-to-end
"Is amoxicillin safe in the third trimester for a UTI?"
01SEARCH
Find the right pages
The system searches our medical library for pages about amoxicillin and pregnancy. It finds 8 useful candidates — including official guidelines from ACOG and Cochrane.
02SHORTLIST
Pick the best 4
Out of those 8 candidates, the system keeps the 4 most relevant ones — the latest pregnancy-care guidelines and antibiotic references.
· ACOG 2023 — pregnancy antibiotics
· Cochrane 2022 — UTI in pregnancy
· WHO AWaRe 2024
· BNF 2024 — amoxicillin
03DRAFT
Write the answer
The LLM writes a draft answer, using only what's in those 4 sources. Every claim gets a citation tag.
Amoxicillin is generally safe in the third trimester for a UTI. Standard dose: 500 mg, 3 times a day, 5–7 days. Confirm with your doctor.
04CHECK
Is each line backed up?
A second model reads each sentence and checks whether the source actually says it. All three sentences pass.

The doseBACKED
"Confirm with doctor"BACKED

Reply to the user

20 · LIMITATIONS
WHAT THIS SYSTEM IS NOT
Honest about what's hard
DocuMed AI helps you think.
It does not replace a doctor.
LIMITATION 01
Mostly English for now
Our medical sources are in English. If a user asks in Nepali, or mixes Nepali and English, retrieval gets weaker.
LIMITATION 02
Not medically certified
DocuMed AI has no regulatory approval. It is for learning and decision support — the doctor still makes the call.
LIMITATION 03
Struggles on rare cases
For rare diseases, children's exceptions, or patients on many drugs, our corpus has fewer documents — so the system often refuses to answer.
LIMITATION 04
Slower than a normal LLM
All the safety checks add time. A DocuMed AI answer takes about 3× longer than a plain LLM. The trade-off: it's grounded.
LIMITATION 05
Only as good as our sources
If a guideline is missing or outdated in our corpus, the answer suffers. Keeping the corpus clean and current is ongoing work.
LIMITATION 06
The verifier isn't perfect
Our NLI checker is good but not flawless. To stay safe, the system leans toward refusing rather than guessing.

22 · BROADER IMPLICATIONS
RESPONSIBLE AI
Why this is bigger than one project
Trust isn't a UX choice.
It's an architecture choice.
Every domain that touches consequence — medicine, law, finance, education — is going to need systems that are willing to say "I don't know." The question isn't whether AI will mediate these interactions. It's whether the AI doing it will have a refusal path.
PRINCIPLE 01
Calibrated humility
A system's confidence should match its evidence — not its fluency.
PRINCIPLE 02
Auditable answers
Every claim points back to evidence. No grounding → no answer.
PRINCIPLE 03
Human-in-the-loop, by default
DocuMed AI is a navigator. The clinician — and the patient — decide.
PRINCIPLE 04
Fail closed, always
In high-stakes systems, silence is a feature, not a bug.
